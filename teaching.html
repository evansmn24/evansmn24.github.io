Teaching & mentoring

Learning mammals in the field

The best way to understand a mammal is to meet it: in a trap, on a camera, through its tracks and signs. I teach with field time, real data and real conservation problems.

01 Philosophy

How I teach

Students learn mammals best with a live trap in their hands and a question in their heads.

Mammalogy works best as an experiential course. Students should leave able to identify their local fauna and also to design a survey, handle animals safely and ethically, and turn field data into management advice. I build courses around those skills: specimen-based lab work for identification and anatomy, field labs with live traps, camera traps and telemetry, and projects that use real monitoring data.

Years of fieldwork in Borneo, often alongside students and local staff, taught me that confidence in the field comes from practice and good mentorship. I bring that same approach to the classroom.

02 Courses

What I teach

Undergraduate · Field

Mammalogy

Evolution, diversity, identification and ecology of mammals, with specimen labs and field sessions using live-trapping, camera traps and sign surveys.

Undergraduate · Field

Wildlife Field Techniques

Capture and handling, telemetry, camera-trap study design and the ethics and safety of working with wild animals.

Graduate

Carnivores in Human-Modified Landscapes

A seminar on movement, health and conflict for carnivores in agricultural and urbanising systems, built around primary literature and student-led analyses.

Graduate

Wildlife Health & Ecotoxicology

Contaminants, physiology and emerging disease in wild populations, connecting individual health to population persistence.

03 Mentoring

Students first

As Assistant Scientific Officer at Danau Girang Field Centre, I worked alongside undergraduate and postgraduate students from Malaysia and around the world on field projects in the Kinabatangan. Student-led camera-trap projects there produced the first activity pattern for the Sunda stink-badger and new data on smooth-coated otter behaviour.

I mentor students to own their projects: framing a question, collecting data safely, analysing it rigorously and publishing it. I'm especially committed to giving students their first field research experience.
